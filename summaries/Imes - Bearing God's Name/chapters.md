### Foreword (Christopher J. H. Wright)

The foreword frames the book as pastorally helpful and theologically rigorous, arguing that the exodus narrative and Sinai formation show the identity of God's people as "name-bearers," and that this is directly relevant to Christian life today.

### Introduction

Imes sets up the core thesis: the third commandment is misunderstood if it is reduced to speech-taboo. She argues it is about bearing God's name-an identity and vocation that continues through Scripture.

### Part I: Becoming the People Who Bear God's Name

#### Chapter 1 - Leaving Egypt

Imes begins with Israel's exodus as the background to Sinai: God's rescue creates a people who now have a new identity. This sets up why the coming "law" is not a prerequisite for salvation but a commissioning for a new vocation.

#### Chapter 2 - Surprised at Sinai

Imes emphasises that Sinai is not where God "starts" with unrelated rules; it is where God claims and forms the people. Sinai is treated as a moment of identity formation that re-frames what Israel is for (God's presence, God's holiness, God's purpose).

#### Chapter 3 - Major Deal

This chapter is presented by reviews as the point where Imes gives the essence of the name command in Exodus 20:7: the command concerns bearing/carrying God's name in ethical and representational ways, illuminated by the high priest's garments and role. It also re-frames the Ten Commandments as wise, life-forming instruction, not merely arbitrary prohibition.

#### Chapter 4 - Now What?

Imes turns from Sinai as an event to Sinai as ongoing formation: what Israel is supposed to become and do as a covenant community. The "what now?" focuses on living as God's named people, where the law functions to shape justice, worship, and communal life. (This "now what?" emphasis is consistent with the book's portrayal of Sinai as the beginning of vocation.)

#### Chapter 5 - Ready to Roll

Imes moves from the formative moment to the forward-looking journey: Israel is prepared to continue, bearing God's name in motion. The wilderness journey functions as a liminal space where identity is reshaped before the promised future.

### Intermission - [Between Parts]

A brief pause to consolidate the shift from "becoming" to "living": readers have been prepared to see that the name theme is not static theology but a lived calling that continues beyond Sinai into Israel's story and the church's mission.

### Part II: Living as the People Who Bear God's Name

#### Chapter 6 - Striking Out

Imes follows Israel as it continues beyond the initial Sinai experience-highlighting how bearing God's name is tested and what happens when the covenant people fail to live as representatives of God's holiness.

#### Chapter 7 - What Yahweh Sees

This chapter develops the "seeing" theme: God's name-bearing people are accountable to a God who sees (knows, discerns, judges) the reality behind actions. This supports the book's ethical insistence that name-bearing is not cosmetic; it is integrity before God.

#### Chapter 8 - Just Give Me Jesus

Imes addresses a common objection or misconception: that the Christian should bypass the Old Testament in favour of Christ alone. Instead, she argues that Jesus does not erase Sinai's meaning; rather, Christ fulfils and advances the vocation to bear God's name and to live holy, representationally truthful lives.

#### Chapter 9 - Blob Tag

A more playful chapter title that signals a return to identity and community markers: name-bearing should be visibly and practically recognisable in how believers live together and interact. The "blob tag" idea aligns with Imes's broader "invisible tattoo"/identity-marker metaphor reported in reviews (identity is permanent, but visible in conduct).

#### Chapter 10 - Who Let You In?

Imes closes the book by focusing on covenant membership and mission: bearing God's name connects to who is included in God's purpose and how the church carries that vocation outward. The emphasis is that name-bearing is not just private morality but a communal, mission-shaped identity.

### Conclusion

The conclusion draws together the argument: Sinai still matters because God forms a people through whom God's name is displayed, and Christians-through Christ-inherit the same vocation of representing God among the nations.
