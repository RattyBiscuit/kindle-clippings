Carmen Joy Imes argues that Christians have commonly misunderstood the third commandment ("You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain") by reducing it to a concern mainly about using God's name as a curse word. Imes instead translates and interprets the command in a fuller, covenantal way: the issue is not merely speech about God, but life that carries God's name - a vocation given to Israel at Sinai and continued as the defining identity of God's people through the rest of Scripture.

**1) Sinai is where Israel's identity is formed**
The book's organising conviction is that Sinai is not an obscure, outdated legal appendix to the Bible; it is the moment where Israel learns who they are by learning whose they are. God rescues them from Egypt, brings them to himself, and commissions them to represent him among the nations. From the start, Imes treats "law" as something more like instruction for holy life than as an arbitrary moral rulebook: Israel's obedience shapes how God's character is displayed to outsiders.

**2) "Taking" God's name means "bearing/carrying" it**
At the heart of Imes's argument is Exodus 20:7. She argues that the Hebrew idea behind "take" is better understood as "bear/lift/carry" rather than "use (in speech)". That interpretive shift matters because it turns the command into a description of vocation: God's people are meant to carry God's name representationally and ethically - through worship, character, and daily conduct.

Imes supports this reading by pointing to the way the high priest "bears/carries" the names of the tribes through the sacred garments (Exodus 28–29). The priest functions as a picture of what the whole covenant community is called to be: a people whose entire embodied life communicates God's name.

**3) "Vain" carries the sense of failure to embody the name rightly**
"Vain" is not only about careless language; it includes bearing God's name in a way that misrepresents him - living like the name is decorative, manageable, or irrelevant rather than holy, true, and authoritative. In other words, God's name is "at stake" in what his people do, not simply in what they say.

**4) The story of Israel becomes the church's story**
Imes traces the theme of bearing God's name through Israel's wilderness journey, Sinai's covenant life, and the later biblical storyline. The goal is continuity: the covenant people formed at Sinai anticipate and feed into what comes later - especially the coming of Christ and the mission of the church.

So, Christians don't read the Old Testament as something to be discarded; they read it as the narrative and theological groundwork for understanding Jesus and for understanding what Christian discipleship really is. The church is not a new "replacement identity" cut off from Israel; rather, it is brought into the same vocation of representing God to the world.

**5) A practical emphasis: law as wisdom for life-as-God's-people**
Throughout the book, Imes consistently highlights that the "law" given at Sinai is bound to God's fatherly character and Israel's calling. It is not merely there to control behaviour; it teaches a way of life that makes God visible - like a people-shaped-by-God community.

A recurring implication is that if believers truly "bear" God's name, then ethics, relationships, worship, and public life are all arenas where God's name is either honoured or dishonoured.

**Two-part structure**
Imes presents this argument in two main movements:

Part I focuses on becoming the people who bear God's name (Sinai as the formative moment, plus what follows as Israel is shaped for its calling).
Part II focuses on living as the people who bear God's name (how the theme develops through Israel's history and then into Christ and the church's mission and ethical life).
The result is a book that reads like a theological journey through the exodus and Sinai, but ends with a direct question to Christian readers: what does it mean, concretely, to be a people whose daily life carries God's name?
