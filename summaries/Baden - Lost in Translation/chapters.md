### Introduction

Baden introduces the problem of "transparent translation": when English biblical words feel obvious but are in fact culturally conditioned. He argues that recognising the gap between ancient meaning and modern assumption is essential for responsible reading. [muse.jhu.edu]

### I. Words Connected to God

#### 1. The Lord

Explores the divine name (YHWH) and how translating it as "the LORD" hides its function as a proper name rather than a title, flattening the personal and political dimensions of Israel’s God.

#### 2. Holy / Sacred

Shows that holy in Hebrew primarily denotes separation or distinctiveness, not moral purity.

#### 3. Glory

Argues that kavod originally referred to weight, substance, or presence, not abstract splendour.

#### 4. Angel

Demonstrates that mal’akh means "messenger", often human rather than supernatural.

#### 5. Satan

Explains that ha-satan is a role or function ("the accuser"), not an independent embodiment of evil.

#### 6. Cherub

Challenges popular imagery by showing cherubim as hybrid, fearsome throne-guardians, not infant-like figures.

#### 7. Ark

Discusses the ark as a portable divine throne, not merely a sacred container.

#### 8. Remember

Emphasises that remembering in Hebrew implies action, not mental recall.

#### 9. Passover

Explores its ritual and political significance beyond later liturgical meanings.

#### 10. Most High

Shows this as a cosmic title rooted in ancient Near Eastern divine hierarchies.

#### Intermezzo: And

Examines the narrative function of waw ("and") and how Hebrew storytelling differs from modern prose conventions.

### II. Words Connected to People

#### 12. Soul

Argues that nefesh means life or living being, not an immortal soul.

#### 13. Spirit

Explores ruach as breath, wind, or vitality, not a separate internal entity.

#### 14. Slave / Servant

Shows how modern moral categories distort ancient social realities of dependency.

#### 15. Vanity

Famously reinterprets hevel (Ecclesiastes) as ephemeral or insubstantial, not narcissism. [patricktreardon.com]

#### 16. Unclean / Impure

Clarifies ritual impurity as a technical state, not sinfulness.

#### 17. Fear

Shows "fear of the Lord" as reverent loyalty, not dread.

#### 18. Kill

Examines legal and moral distinctions obscured by English translations.

#### 19. Leprosy

Explains that biblical ṣara‘at is not Hansen’s disease, but a category of ritual affliction.

#### 20. Levite

Explores evolving social and cultic roles.

#### 21. Hebrew

Shows it as a social or outsider designation, not an ethnic label in the modern sense.

#### 22. Sons of

Explains Semitic idioms of belonging and identity.

#### 23. Hell

Demonstrates that Sheol is the grave or underworld, not a place of eternal punishment.

#### 24. Heart

Shows the heart as the centre of thought and decision, not emotion alone.

#### 25. Stranger

Reinterprets biblical hospitality laws in social-contextual terms.

#### 26. Behold

Explores this term’s role in guiding attention and narrative emphasis.

### III. Words That Connect God and People

#### 27. Love

Explains love as loyalty and obligation, not primarily emotion.

#### 28. Atone

Connects atonement with ritual repair, not substitutionary punishment.

#### 29. Law

Shows torah as instruction or guidance, not legal code.

#### 30. Lovingkindness

Unpacks ḥesed as covenantal loyalty rather than sentimentality.

#### 31. Righteousness

Defines righteousness as right relationship and order, not moral perfection.

#### 32. Ban / Devotion / Destruction

Explores ḥerem as ritual dedication, with unsettling ethical implications.

#### 33. Grace

Shows grace as favour within hierarchy, not abstract benevolence.

#### 34. Sacrifice

Reframes sacrifice as gift and hospitality, not appeasement.

#### 35. Redeem

Explains redemption as familial and economic responsibility.

#### 36. Covenant

Shows covenants as binding relational frameworks, not contracts.

#### 37. Sabbath

Explores Sabbath as a cosmic and social institution, not just rest.

#### 38. And It Came to Pass

Explains how formulaic phrases shape biblical narrative rhythms.

### Postscript: Bible

Baden closes by reminding readers that "the Bible" itself is a modern category, and that recognising its composite, historical nature deepens rather than diminishes its significance.
