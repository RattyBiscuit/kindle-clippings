The book consists of 39 short, thematically grouped essays, each devoted to a familiar English biblical word or phrase whose meaning has shifted significantly through translation, interpretation, and cultural change over the past two millennia. Baden focuses primarily on the Hebrew Bible (Old Testament) and demonstrates how modern readers often misunderstand biblical concepts by assuming modern or theological meanings that were foreign to the original Hebrew context.

Baden's central thesis is that translation is never neutral. Words that seem transparent in English - such as law, angel, soul, love, hell, or grace - carry layers of historical interpretation that obscure their original sense in ancient Israelite culture. These shifts are not merely linguistic but ideological, shaped by later Jewish, Christian, and Western intellectual traditions.

Rather than criticising translators or translation traditions, Baden shows that meaning is inevitably filtered through culture, and that modern readers often read theological assumptions back into ancient texts. His aim is to recover what these words meant to their earliest audiences, not to prescribe how they must be used today

The book is deliberately non-technical, each chapter is a concise essay that combines Hebrew philology, historical context, and close reading of biblical passages. Collectively, the essays argue that appreciating the strangeness of the biblical past allows for a more honest and nuanced engagement with scripture in the present.
