**Chapter I** contrasts restoring Gospel Jewishness with “contextualising” the Gospel, arguing the latter approach is misguided.

**Chapter II** forms the book's core, exploring what transforms when Jewishness is restored to the Gospel. This restoration means presenting the Gospel's complete content regarding Jews and Jewish-Church relations, offering “the whole counsel of God” rather than partial truth1. Stern advocates understanding New Testament texts as first-century hearers would have understood them1, though contemporary application requires contextual adaptation.

**Chapter III** establishes three foundational presuppositions: Christianity is Jewish, antisemitism contradicts Christian faith, and neglecting Jewish evangelism constitutes antisemitism.

**Chapter IV** examines blessings flowing from restoring Gospel Jewishness for both Church and Jewish people1. The Church receives blessings through Genesis 12:3's principle that those blessing Abraham's people receive blessings themselves1, whilst Jewish acceptance will bring “life from the dead”—ultimately the Resurrection itself, contingent upon “all Israel” being saved.
