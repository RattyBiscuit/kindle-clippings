### Chapter 1: A New Perspective on Käsemann? Apocalyptic, Covenant, and the Righteousness of God

The opening chapter examines the work of the influential German New Testament scholar Ernst Käsemann, particularly his emphasis on Paul's apocalyptic outlook. Wright uses the discussion to address one of the major questions in Pauline interpretation: **should Paul's theology primarily be understood through the categories of apocalyptic expectation, or through the story of God's covenant with Israel?**

Wright argues that the two approaches should not be treated as mutually exclusive. Paul's apocalyptic vision makes sense precisely because he believes that Israel's God has acted decisively within history. The death and resurrection of Jesus represent the moment when God's long-promised purposes have broken into the present age.

A key issue is the meaning of **God's righteousness**. Wright argues against reducing it simply to a quality that God possesses or a legal status that God gives to individuals. God's righteousness is closely connected with his covenant faithfulness - God is acting faithfully towards his promises to Abraham and Israel.

This has major implications for Paul's understanding of salvation. Salvation is not simply about individuals escaping punishment or acquiring a favourable legal status before God. It is about God's covenant purposes being fulfilled through the Messiah and extending to the nations.

Wright therefore connects several ideas that are often separated:

- God's covenant with Israel
- Israel's Scriptures
- God's righteousness
- the Messiah
- the resurrection
- the inclusion of Gentiles
- the renewal of creation

The chapter establishes an important principle for the rest of the book: **Paul's theology needs to be interpreted within the large biblical narrative rather than extracted into a collection of isolated doctrines.**

### Chapter 2: Justification by (Covenantal) Faith to the (Covenantal) Doers: Romans 2 within the Argument of the Letter

This chapter focuses on Romans 2 and particularly on the difficult question of Paul's discussion of those who "do" what the law requires.

Wright challenges interpretations that make Romans 2 appear to contradict Paul's teaching elsewhere about justification by faith. He argues that the chapter needs to be understood within the argument of Romans as a whole.

The central issue is the Jewish-Gentile problem. Paul is asking who belongs to God's covenant family now that the Messiah has come. The question is therefore not simply, "How can an individual sinner get to heaven?" It is also, **"Who are God's covenant people?"**

Wright places particular emphasis on the covenantal dimensions of Paul's language. Faith is not merely an intellectual act or a private psychological experience. Nor is "works" simply a synonym for human attempts to earn salvation.

Instead, Paul is dealing with the identity and vocation of God's people.

The chapter therefore contributes to Wright's broader understanding of justification. Justification has a **law-court dimension**, but it is also connected with God's covenant and the public declaration of who belongs to his people.

The important distinction is between:

1. earning salvation through good works, which Wright rejects; and
2. the obedient life expected of those who belong to God's covenant family.

This interpretation helps explain why Paul can insist upon faith while simultaneously speaking positively about obedience, holiness and the final judgement according to works.

Wright's treatment also reinforces his contention that justification cannot be separated from Paul's larger story about Israel, the Messiah and the Gentile mission.

### Chapter 3: How and Why Paul Invented "Christian Theology"

This is one of the book's most conceptually important essays. Wright asks what it actually means to describe Paul as a "theologian".

His argument is that Paul did something historically remarkable. Paul inherited Jewish monotheism, Israel's Scriptures and Jewish expectations concerning God's future, but he had to rethink all three around the unexpected reality of **Jesus as Israel's Messiah and risen Lord**.

Paul therefore did not simply take an existing Christian theological system and explain it. The Christian theological task was being created as Paul responded to the historical event of Jesus.

Wright argues that Paul's theology emerged from several overlapping convictions:

- the God of Israel is the one true God;
- Israel's Scriptures tell the story of God's purposes;
- Jesus is Israel's Messiah;
- Jesus has been raised from the dead;
- the Holy Spirit is active in the present;
- the Gentiles are being incorporated into God's people;
- God's ultimate purpose is the renewal of creation.

The resurrection is particularly important. If Jesus has been raised, Paul's understanding of God's future has effectively begun in advance.

This produces what Wright sees as a distinctive form of Christian reasoning. Paul takes ancient Jewish beliefs and **reconfigures them around Jesus** without abandoning their fundamental foundations.

The chapter therefore presents Paul not as a detached systematic theologian but as a thinker engaged in a theological emergency created by the events surrounding Jesus.

Paul's theology is consequently both intellectual and pastoral. He is not constructing theology for its own sake. He is using theology to answer practical questions about worship, ethics, community, mission, suffering and Christian identity.

---

### Chapter 4: How Greek Was Paul's Eschatology?

Wright turns to Paul's understanding of the future and challenges the assumption that Paul's eschatology can simply be categorised as either "Jewish" or "Greek".

The underlying question is how Paul's expectation of resurrection, judgement and the future renewal of creation relates to the intellectual world of the Greek-speaking Mediterranean.

Wright argues that Paul's eschatology remains fundamentally rooted in **Jewish biblical hope**, even though Paul communicates that hope in a Greek-speaking world.

This distinction is crucial. Paul uses Greek philosophical and rhetorical language because that is the linguistic environment in which he operates. But the content of his hope is not simply borrowed from Greek philosophy.

The Christian hope of resurrection is particularly significant. Wright emphasises that resurrection, in Paul's sense, is not merely the continuation of the soul after death. It involves the **bodily resurrection of the dead**.

This leads into one of Wright's recurring themes: Christianity's ultimate hope is not escape from creation but **renewed creation**.

The resurrection of Jesus is the prototype and beginning of this future reality. What happened to Jesus is therefore a preview of what God intends ultimately to do with his people and, ultimately, with creation itself.

The chapter helps establish Wright's wider argument that Paul's theology cannot be understood properly if the Jewish narrative of creation, covenant, exile, Messiah and restoration is replaced with a generic Greek conception of the afterlife.

---

### Chapter 5: Paul's Western Missionary Project: Jerusalem, Rome, Spain in Historical and Theological Perspective

This chapter examines Paul's plans to travel from Jerusalem through Rome towards Spain.

Wright argues that Paul's geographical strategy was not incidental. His missionary journeys expressed his theological conviction that the good news concerning Israel's Messiah was now being announced to the nations.

Rome occupies a particularly interesting position. Paul is not simply trying to establish another Christian congregation. Rome is the heart of the imperial world, and Paul's gospel announces **Jesus rather than Caesar as Lord**.

Spain, meanwhile, represents the western edge of Paul's intended mission. Wright examines the historical and theological reasons for Paul's plans and asks what they tell us about his understanding of his apostolic vocation.

Jerusalem remains crucial because Paul's mission to the Gentiles is not presented as a rejection of Israel. Rather, Gentile inclusion represents the fulfilment of Israel's Scriptures.

The collection for Jerusalem also becomes important. Paul's Gentile churches are not simply separate Christian communities scattered across the Roman Empire. They are being integrated into the renewed people of God.

Thus Paul's missionary project has several dimensions:

- geographical expansion;
- fulfilment of Scripture;
- unity between Jewish and Gentile Christians;
- the announcement of Jesus as Lord;
- the establishment of new communities;
- the extension of God's kingdom.

Mission, in Wright's interpretation, is therefore inseparable from Paul's theology.

---

### Chapter 6: The Glory Returns: Spirit, Temple and Eschatology in Paul and John

This chapter explores the relationship between the **Holy Spirit, the Temple and eschatology** in Paul and the Gospel of John.

Wright draws attention to the biblical theme of God's glory and presence. In the Old Testament, the Temple represents the place where God's presence dwells among his people. Yet Israel's story also includes the departure of God's glory and the expectation that God's presence will return.

Wright argues that Paul and John understand the coming of the Spirit in ways that resonate strongly with this Temple narrative.

The extraordinary implication is that God's presence is no longer restricted to a physical Temple. Through the Spirit, God's presence is experienced among and within his people.

This gives Paul's teaching about the church a much greater theological significance.

The church is not merely an association of people who share religious beliefs. The community itself becomes a **Temple-like dwelling place of God's presence**.

The chapter consequently brings together:

- Temple theology;
- God's glory;
- the Holy Spirit;
- the resurrection;
- the Messiah;
- the church;
- eschatological fulfilment.

The "return" of God's glory therefore forms part of Wright's understanding of the new age inaugurated through Jesus and the Spirit.

---

### Chapter 7: Historical Paul and "Systematic Theology": To Start a Discussion

Wright turns directly to the relationship between historical research and systematic theology.

He asks whether it is legitimate to construct Christian doctrine from Paul's letters while simultaneously attempting to reconstruct the historical Paul.

His answer is essentially yes, but with significant qualifications.

Wright argues that historical Paul and theological Paul should not be separated artificially. The historical Paul was himself engaged in theological interpretation. His letters emerged from real historical circumstances, but the arguments within those letters are theological arguments.

This means that good Christian theology should pay close attention to:

- historical context;
- Paul's Jewish background;
- the circumstances of individual letters;
- Paul's use of Scripture;
- the development of his thought;
- the concrete problems facing his churches.

At the same time, Wright resists the idea that historical scholarship should simply replace theology.

Theology remains necessary because Paul himself was doing theology. The task of contemporary Christians is therefore not merely to reproduce Paul's first-century formulations but to engage in a comparable theological task in a different historical situation.

This chapter is especially important for understanding Wright's overall method. He wants **history, exegesis and theology to work together rather than compete with one another**.

---

### Chapter 8: Paul and Missional Hermeneutics

Here Wright moves from interpreting Paul to considering **how Paul should be read by the contemporary church**.

"Missional hermeneutics" means reading Scripture in a way that recognises God's mission and the church's participation in it.

Wright argues that Paul's letters cannot properly be understood apart from his missionary purpose. Paul is not writing detached theological essays. He is announcing the gospel, forming communities and teaching them how to live as God's renewed people.

This affects how Christians should read his letters today.

The reader should therefore ask not only:

> What does Paul teach?

but also:

> What is Paul trying to accomplish in this community?

and:

> How does this contribute to God's mission?

Wright's approach makes Paul's theology inherently practical. Doctrine, worship, ethics and mission belong together.

The church's task is not simply to preserve correct beliefs. It is to embody the reality announced by the gospel.

Paul's writings therefore challenge contemporary churches to think of themselves as **communities participating in God's redemptive mission**.

---

### Chapter 9: God Put Jesus Forth: Reflections on Romans 3:24-26

This chapter concentrates on one of the most debated passages in Paul's writings: Romans 3:24-26.

Wright examines Paul's language concerning God's action through Jesus, particularly the meaning of God's righteousness, justification, redemption and the death of Christ.

The passage is often interpreted primarily through the framework of **penal substitution**, with Jesus receiving the punishment deserved by sinners.

Wright does not deny the importance of the cross as dealing decisively with sin. However, he argues that the passage must be understood within Paul's broader biblical and covenantal framework.

The cross demonstrates God's righteousness because God is acting faithfully to his covenant promises and dealing decisively with the problem of human sin.

The chapter therefore connects the cross with:

- God's covenant;
- Israel's Scriptures;
- God's righteousness;
- the problem of sin;
- the Messiah;
- God's faithfulness;
- the inclusion of Gentiles.

Wright's reading does not make justification unimportant. Instead, it places justification inside the larger narrative.

The fundamental question becomes not merely "How can an individual be forgiven?" but **"How has God demonstrated his covenant faithfulness and created a new people through the Messiah?"**

Romans 3 thus becomes a concentrated statement of Paul's gospel.

---

### Chapter 10: A Poem Doubled: Pauline Reflections on Theology and Poetry

This essay explores Paul's use of poetry, particularly the poetic and hymnic material embedded within his letters.

Wright is interested in the relationship between **theological truth and poetic expression**.

Paul's most important theological claims are not always expressed through abstract propositions. At times, he uses poetry, hymns, prayers and carefully structured language to communicate the meaning of Christ and the Christian life.

This is significant because poetry allows Paul to communicate theology through imagination, rhythm, narrative and worship.

The chapter therefore challenges a purely propositional understanding of theology.

For Paul, theology can be:

- sung;
- prayed;
- celebrated;
- narrated;
- embodied;
- remembered.

This also illustrates how closely Paul connects theology with worship. Theological reflection is not simply something Christians do in classrooms. It shapes how they praise God and understand their identity.

Wright consequently presents Paul's poetry as a form of theological reasoning in its own right.

---

### Chapter 11: The Challenge of Fraternity in Paul

This chapter considers the social implications of Paul's theology, particularly the problem of **how people who are divided by ethnicity, social status and cultural background can become one family**.

The word "fraternity" is important because Paul repeatedly describes Christian communities using family-like language.

The early Christian movement consisted of people who would normally have remained socially separate. Jews and Gentiles, slaves and free people, men and women, wealthy and poor people were being brought together within the same communities.

Wright argues that this was not simply an attractive social ideal. It was a theological consequence of the gospel.

If there is one God, one Messiah, one Spirit and one baptism, then those who belong to Christ form a new family.

This creates serious practical demands.

Christian unity cannot remain an abstract doctrine. It must become visible in:

- shared meals;
- mutual care;
- generosity;
- reconciliation;
- worship;
- acceptance;
- willingness to surrender status and privilege.

Paul's vision of the church therefore challenges communities that reproduce the divisions of wider society.

The gospel creates a radically different social reality in which **Christian identity transcends ethnic and social boundaries**.

---

### Chapter 12: Learning from Paul Together: How New Insights into Paul's Teaching Can Help Move Us Forward in Mission

The final chapter draws together many of the themes developed throughout the book and asks what contemporary Christians can learn from renewed study of Paul.

Wright does not suggest that scholarship exists simply to settle academic disputes. The purpose of better interpretation is ultimately to help the church understand and live out its vocation.

Several themes come together:

- Paul's Jewish context matters.
- The resurrection is central.
- God's covenant faithfulness is fundamental.
- Justification concerns God's declaration concerning his covenant people.
- The Spirit represents God's presence with his people.
- The church is a new community created around the Messiah.
- Eschatology concerns renewed creation rather than escape from creation.
- Mission flows from God's purposes for Israel and the nations.
- Theology and practical Christian living cannot be separated.

Wright's final emphasis is therefore communal. Christians should learn from Paul **together**, rather than treating Pauline interpretation merely as an individual intellectual exercise.

The church needs serious biblical and theological reflection because its understanding of Scripture shapes its mission.

The final essay consequently turns the whole book outward. Studying Paul should result not simply in a better understanding of Paul but in a better understanding of **what the church is, what it is for, and how it should participate in God's mission**.

## Overall summary of the book

The central argument running through _Interpreting Paul_ can be summarised as follows:

**Paul's theology makes the most sense when it is understood as the Jewish story of Israel's God, covenant, Messiah and future kingdom being reinterpreted around the historical event of Jesus' death and resurrection.**

For Wright, Paul did not abandon Judaism and invent an unrelated religion. Instead, Paul believed that the God of Israel had fulfilled his promises in an unexpected way through Jesus.

That conviction transformed Paul's understanding of virtually everything.

### 1. Israel's story is essential

Paul's thought begins with the God of Israel and Israel's Scriptures. Abraham, Moses, the Exodus, the covenant, the Temple, exile and restoration all form part of the conceptual world in which Paul operates.

Consequently, Wright argues against interpretations that begin with abstract philosophical or systematic categories and only subsequently bring Paul into the discussion.

### 2. Jesus is the climax of the story

The decisive event is Jesus.

Paul believes that Jesus is Israel's Messiah, that he died as the decisive act of God's saving purpose, and that God raised him from the dead.

The resurrection means that the future has already begun.

### 3. Eschatology is central

Wright sees Paul's theology as fundamentally eschatological.

Paul believes that God's new age has begun in Jesus and the Spirit, even though the final renewal of creation has not yet occurred.

This creates the characteristic Christian tension between:

- what God has already accomplished; and
- what God will ultimately accomplish.

Christians therefore live between resurrection and final resurrection, between the inauguration of God's kingdom and its consummation.

### 4. Justification is covenantal and ecclesial

Wright's understanding of justification is one of the book's most important themes.

He argues that justification should not be reduced to an abstract mechanism by which an isolated individual receives a particular legal status.

It is also God's declaration concerning **who belongs to the covenant family**.

Faith in the Messiah therefore has consequences for the identity and composition of God's people.

This explains why Gentile inclusion is so important to Paul.

### 5. The gospel creates a new humanity

The gospel is not simply a message about individual salvation.

It creates a new community in which historic divisions are overcome.

Jew and Gentile are incorporated into one family because they share the same Messiah, Spirit and Lord.

This is why Paul's theology has such strong social implications.

### 6. The Holy Spirit signals God's renewed presence

The Spirit is not merely an internal source of personal religious experience.

The Spirit represents the presence of God among his people and signals the arrival of the promised new age.

The church consequently becomes a community in which God's presence is manifested.

### 7. Mission belongs to the heart of Paul's theology

Paul's missionary activity is not an optional practical application added after the theology.

Mission flows directly from Paul's conviction that Israel's God is fulfilling his promises and bringing the nations into his renewed family.

Paul's journeys, his letters, his collection for Jerusalem and his plans for Rome and Spain all belong to this larger theological project.

### 8. Theology should produce transformed communities

For Wright, correct interpretation cannot stop with intellectual understanding.

Paul's theology was intended to shape communities.

A church that understands Paul's gospel should therefore become:

- worshipping;
- holy;
- united;
- reconciled;
- generous;
- outward-looking;
- missionary;
- centred on Jesus;
- empowered by the Spirit.
