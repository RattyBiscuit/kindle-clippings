> We might note, both to affirm and radically to modify one normal reading of Paul's 'atonement' theology, that this is both penal (katakrima . . . katekrinen can hardly be anything but penal) and substitutionary (the sentence of condemnation has been both passed and carried out in the Messiah's flesh, therefore the Messiah's people are not condemned), but that Paul does not say here that God condemned the Messiah. He condemned sin in the Messiah's flesh, the place where (we might say, echoing 5.20-21) sin had grown to full height through the law, so that in the place where sin increased, grace might increase all the more. This is what happens when we allow the covenantal and apocalyptic meanings, not to replace forensic ones, but to hold them in proper (and biblical) context and balance.

It says he condemned sin in the messiah's flesh, where in Romans does he get this from?

> For what was impossible for the law, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did. By sending his own Son **in the likeness of sinful flesh and concerning sin, he condemned sin in the flesh**, in order that the requirement of the law would be fulfilled in us, who do not live according to the flesh but according to the Spirit.  ([Romans 8:3–4](https://ref.ly/Ro8.3-4;leb), LEB)




> The reference to God condemning sin in the Messiah’s flesh comes from [Romans 8:3–4](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bleb2.66.8.3-66.8.4)[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f379433). In that passage, Paul writes that God “sent his own son in the likeness of sinful flesh, and as a sin-offering; and, right there in the flesh, he condemned sin.”[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f90786)
> 
> Wright notes that [Romans 8:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bleb2.66.8.1) directly answers the condemnation language introduced in chapter 2, with the announcement that “there is no condemnation for those in the Messiah, Jesus”[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f89777). The condemnation (katakrima) announced in chapter 2 has been dealt with and replaced with a positive verdict of righteousness (dikaiōma)[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f90093).
> 
> The crucial point is that Paul speaks of God condemning sin itself—not individual sinners—as a penal act, and this condemnation occurs in the Messiah’s flesh rather than being directed at the Messiah personally[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f91340). Sin is condemned in the Messiah’s flesh precisely as the place where sin had reached its full power through the law[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f92101). This framework allows Wright to argue that the event is simultaneously penal and substitutionary—the sentence is passed and executed in the Messiah’s flesh, so his people escape condemnation—while maintaining that God condemned sin, not the Messiah himself[<sup>1</sup>](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTERPPAULWRIGHT3?pos=res%2fLLS%3aINTERPPAULWRIGHT3%2f2020-06-30T21%3a34%3a47Z%2f91688).

> It is in Romans 8 that he states most fully both the role of the spirit and the relation of the moral life to final justification. To final justification, we note; because, though we have not here expounded it, in between Romans 2 and Romans 8 comes, of course, 3.21-4.25, and indeed 5.1-11, in which Paul makes it clear beyond any cavil that those who believe the gospel are reckoned, in the present time (3.21, 26), to be 'in the right'. Justification by faith in the present truly anticipates the final verdict and thus provides the foundation for the united believing community without the boundary-markers of Jewish life.

Justification is now, not the future. The result is that now we can identify with either the old nature or with the spirit (Romans 8: 5-6).

> For those who are living according to the flesh are intent on the things of the flesh, but those who are living according to the Spirit are intent on the things of the Spirit. For the mindset of the flesh is death, but the mindset of the Spirit is life and peace ([Romans 8:5–6](https://ref.ly/Ro8.5-6;leb), LEB)

The moral life that is desired by God for us to live is now possible through our active identification; who am I becomes the question. Who is the Lord over me, who decides what is good and evil? Do I continue in the ways of the old nature, being under myself as God and thus under the powers of evil, or do l go the way of the spirit, like in psalm 1:

> Blessed is the one who does not… walk in the way of sinners…
> But his delight is in the Torah (instruction, law) of Yahweh.

> That same spirit will now complete the task by producing, in the present time, not indeed a complete legalistic obedience to every jot and tittle of Torah (Paul frequently stresses that point), nor indeed, as some today want to argue, a renewed Torah-observance which would include a pure-food code, sabbath-keeping and so on,19 but a way of life which corresponds to the divine intention of the life-giving Torah.
