N. T. Wright's _Interpreting Paul_ is a collection of twelve essays rather than a conventional, continuously argued book. Published in 2020 as part of Wright's collected essays, it brings together studies written largely after _Paul and the Faithfulness of God_, and therefore represents a mature development of his understanding of Paul. The essays address Paul's theology, eschatology, justification, mission, ecclesiology, use of Scripture, the relationship between history and theology, and the practical implications of Paul's thought for the contemporary church.

The book's central concern is **how Paul should be understood within the Jewish and biblical story that he believed had reached its climax in Jesus Christ**. Wright consistently resists readings that detach Paul's theology from Israel's Scriptures, the Jewish context of the first century, the resurrection of Jesus, and the expectation of God's renewal of creation.

A particularly important feature of the book is Wright's insistence that Paul was not merely producing an abstract system of doctrines. Paul was interpreting what God had done in Jesus and was working out the consequences of that event for God's people, the nations, the church, and the whole created order. For Wright, Paul's theology is therefore simultaneously **historical, biblical, eschatological, ecclesial and missional**.

The twelve chapters cover topics ranging from the debate with Ernst Käsemann and the meaning of God's righteousness, through Romans, Paul's eschatology and missionary strategy, to the Temple, the Holy Spirit, Christian theology, mission, poetry and the social implications of Paul's teaching.
